=== Session Facilitation Notes

* ★ This session is most important for participants who feel the strongest pressure to "design everything perfectly."
* ★ Confusion between Engineering Risk and PM Risk comes up frequently. Make it clear that "the timeline is tight" is a PM Risk.
* ★ Some participants will find it difficult to fill in the PART 4 "doesn't need to be done now" column. Emphasize that it means "planning when to revisit" — not "never doing it."
* ★ Make sure to cover the "design simplification flywheel" at the end. This is the bridge to Session 8.

=== 2. Concept Explanation (22 minutes)

==== Concept 1 — Quality Attributes vs Functionality (7 minutes)

The key distinction must be pinned down precisely first.

----
Functionality
─────────────
"What the system does"
Examples: Sends a notification, assigns a task, shows a list

→ Can be accumulated sprint by sprint
→ Relatively easy to add later


Quality Attributes (the "-ilities")
────────────────────────────────────
"How well the system does it"
Examples: Performance, Availability, Security,
          Scalability, Modifiability

→ Secured through architectural decisions
→ Very expensive to change later
→ Unlike functionality, they trade off against each other
----

Why this distinction matters:

----
Cost of adding functionality later:          Linear
Cost of adding a Quality Attribute later:    Exponential
----

Adding Consistency later could require changing the DB schema, API structure, and cache strategy entirely. If considered from the start, a single design decision might have been enough.

Quality Attributes in the TeamOrder case:

[cols="1,1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Quality Attribute | Meaning in TeamOrder | Risk if neglected
| _Reliability_ | Night-shift urgent notifications must definitely arrive | Line stoppage
| _Performance_ | Notification delivery delay must be within acceptable range | Team member is slow to respond
| _Modifiability_ | Notification channels must be addable later | Full rework when adding email/SMS
| _Usability_ | Must be easy to confirm in the field | Notification received but no action taken
|===


Weinberg's Attributes (Session 2) reappear here. Attributes not surfaced in Session 2 become design risks in Session 7.

==== Concept 2 — Risk-Driven Model (Fairbanks) (10 minutes)

Fairbanks's central question:

[quote]
____
_"How much software architecture should you do?"_
____

His answer: _In proportion to the risk._

----
Risk-Driven Model — 3-step loop

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                 │
│  1. Identify failure risks                      │
│     "If this fails, does the project die?"      │
│                                                 │
│           ↓                                     │
│                                                 │
│  2. Choose techniques matched to the risk       │
│     Engineering risk → only Engineering         │
│     techniques are effective                    │
│                                                 │
│           ↓                                     │
│                                                 │
│  3. Stop when risk is sufficiently reduced      │
│     "Just Enough" — design is a means,         │
│     not an end in itself                        │
│                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
----

_Engineering Risk vs Project Management Risk_

This distinction is the most practical part for engineers at 3–4 years of experience.

----
Engineering Risk                   Project Management Risk
─────────────────────────────────  ───────────────────────────────────
Problems of system analysis/       Problems of schedule, team size,
design/implementation              and priorities

"Performance won't meet           "We probably can't finish in 3 weeks"
 requirements"
"Can't guarantee data             "The team doesn't know this
 consistency"                      technology"
"There's a security               "Requirements keep changing"
 vulnerability"

→ Resolved with Engineering        → Resolved with PM techniques
  techniques                         (schedule adjustment, training,
  (prototype, load testing,           process improvement, etc.)
   threat modeling, etc.)
----

_When risk type ≠ technique type, the technique is ineffective:_

Drawing class diagrams for a performance risk is ineffective. Performance risks require techniques like load testing or queuing analysis. Drawing architecture diagrams for a schedule risk is also ineffective.

_Practical risk identification checklist:_

----
If any of the following are "YES," treat as an Engineering Risk

□ Does this system have demanding response time requirements?
□ Is data consistency critical to this system?
□ Does it handle security-sensitive data?
□ Is there a possibility of sudden traffic growth?
□ Do multiple teams touch the same component?
□ Does a failure in one component cascade to full system failure?
□ Are significant feature additions/changes expected later?
□ Is integration with external systems/services complex?
----

'''

==== Concept 3 — Connection to Cunningham (5 minutes)

Point out where the Risk-Driven Model and Session 5's Cunningham meet.

Fairbanks's "stop when risk is reduced" shares the same spirit as Cunningham's "What is the simplest thing that could possibly work?"

----
Low-risk areas:
  Elaborate design is actually wasteful
  → Cunningham: Simplest Thing
  → Fairbanks: Stop designing

High-risk areas:
  Insufficient design is the cause of failure
  → Fairbanks: Choose techniques matched to risk
  → Cunningham: Explore quickly with Spike
----

Conclusion: _"How much to design" is judged not by "how complete do we want it" but by "where are the failure risks."_

=== 5. Debrief (10 minutes)

_Facilitator guide — anticipated resistance and responses_

----
"You can't know all the risks in advance."
────────────────────────────────────────────
True. You can't know them completely.
But keep two things in mind.

First, focusing on the risks you can know is where to start.
Risks you can't know are discovered and addressed during operation.

Second, Session 6's analogical reasoning helps here.
Systems with similar structures tend to produce familiar risks —
"Prototypical Risks." Knowing recurring risk patterns from
the same type of problem reduces blind spots.

"How do you define Quality Attributes numerically?"
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
We come back to Session 2's Attributes.
Quantifying "response time" as a Quality Attribute
as "95th percentile under 200ms" is exactly what
Session 3's Context-Free Questions extracted.
An Attribute without a number cannot be verified.

"Won't things we skip now become harder later?"
──────────────────────────────────────────────────
They might. That's why PART 4 asks "when will we revisit this."
"Not doing it now" is not "never doing it."
Monitor for when the risk grows,
and re-invest at that point.
----

_The Design Simplification Flywheel_

----
One more key outcome of the Risk-Driven approach to cover before closing.

The clearer it becomes what "doesn't need to be done now,"
the more you can focus on "what must be done now."

Focus enables → completing that faster.
Completing faster enables → delivering to users sooner.
Delivering sooner enables → receiving real feedback sooner.
Having feedback enables → knowing more precisely what to build next.

Problem Space exploration and Risk-Driven design may seem like
they slow things down, but they actually make this flywheel possible.

Of course, deferred things eventually come back.
But by then — the core is already in users' hands.
And extending a validated core is far safer
than building everything from scratch without validation.
----
