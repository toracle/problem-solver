=== 2. Concept Explanation (20 minutes)

==== Concept 1 — Context-Free Questions (12 minutes)

A concept Weinberg presents in _Exploring Requirements_.
Context-Free Questions are _general-purpose questions_ applicable to any domain or product. Because they presuppose no specific solution, they allow exploration without narrowing the problem space.

They fall into three categories.

_① Process Questions — How is it happening?_

Questions to understand the current situation and flow.

----
"How are you currently handling this problem?"
"Who is doing this task? How many people?"
"When does this problem feel most acute?"
"What happens if this isn't resolved?"
"Is there a workaround you're using right now?"
----

The last question in particular — _"What workaround are you using right now?"_ — is what Weinberg emphasizes as the most powerful. A workaround is a clue to the real need. People are already doing in action what they cannot express in words.

_② Product Questions — What should it be?_

Questions to understand expectations and boundaries for the system.

----
"What must this system absolutely do?"
"What must it never do?"
"What state would let you say 'this worked'?"
"What single missing thing would be most valuable?"
"Who judges whether this was successful?"
----

"What must it never do?" is particularly important. It rarely surfaces in requirements conversations, but it frequently becomes the Constraint that trips things up later.

_③ Meta-Questions — Questions about the questioning_

The category Weinberg often describes as the most powerful.

----
"Would you be okay with me asking you questions like this?"
"Is there anything I might be missing?"
"Can I check that I've understood correctly?"
"Could you connect me with someone who knows more about this?"
"Are any of my questions heading in the wrong direction?"
----

Why Meta-Questions are powerful: they give the other person _permission to correct the direction of the conversation_. When an engineer is operating under a wrong assumption, the other person can naturally correct it.

'''

==== Concept 2 — Black Box Test (8 minutes)

If Context-Free Questions have been used to explore sufficiently, the next step is agreeing on the _boundary_ of the system.

Weinberg describes this as the _Black Box Test_ — agreeing first on only what is visible from the outside, before discussing how to implement the inside.

----
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                             │
│  Trigger ──→ [   Black Box   ] ──→ Output   │
│                                             │
│                  ↕ Edge conditions          │
│                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
----

_Black Box Template:_

[cols="1,1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Item | Question | Notes
| _Name_ | What do you call this feature/system in one word? | Agreeing on a name is the first alignment
| _Trigger_ | When does this feature start? | What event activates it?
| _Input_ | What goes in? Who provides it? | People, data, system events
| _Output_ | What must come out? To whom? | User screen, data, external system
| _Edge Cases_ | Conditions where it fails or doesn't work | The part most often omitted
|===


Why the Black Box Test is useful:
* Boundary can be agreed on without debating implementation
* "What happens in this case?" edge case questions surface naturally from the boundary
* Requirements can be confirmed in language both BizDev and users understand

'''

==== Brief Connection — Jobs-to-be-Done (supplementary concept, 3 minutes)

Deliver Christensen's perspective in one line.

[quote]
____
_"People don't buy products. They 'hire' products to accomplish a Job."_
____

"Please build a notification feature" is a _feature request_.
"Please eliminate the situation where the team lead assigns but the team member doesn't know" is a _Job_.

The same Job can be completed in multiple ways. It might not require notifications at all. Context-Free Questions are the tool for finding the Job behind a feature request.

=== 3. Fixed Case Exercise (30 minutes)


==== Small Group Share Guide

_Small Group Share (5 minutes)_

Groups that used the same role card compare their Black Boxes.

Facilitator guide:
----
Comparison Points
──────────────────
"Same interview subject — how different are the Black Boxes?"
  → The direction of the questions was different.
    Trace backwards: which question led to which discovery?

"Which team found the most edge cases — what questions did they ask?"
  → Teams that asked "what should it never do?" and
    "under what conditions does this fail?" find more edge cases.

"What happened in teams that used a Meta-Question?"
  → 'Is there anything I might have missed?' pulls out the most new information.
    Bring this experience into the full group share.
----

=== 5. Debrief (10 minutes)

_Facilitator guide — anticipated resistance and responses_

----
"Context-Free Questions feel too basic.
 Wouldn't it be strange to ask these in real work?"
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
True. The goal is not to recite these questions verbatim.
The goal is to hold these categories in mind
and get this information naturally within normal conversation.
Just as Seunghyun didn't say to Minjun
"I'll now ask you Process Questions."

"What if BizDev says they don't know?"
────────────────────────────────────────
'I don't know' is also information.
'Who do you think would know?' is the next question.
And if nobody knows — that itself is the topic of Session 5.
It may be Essential Ambiguity.

"There are too many edge cases in the Black Box. We're stuck."
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
That's a good sign. Finding more edge cases means fewer surprises later.
You don't have to resolve all of them.
'We now know about this' is what matters.

"I'm not good at interviewing. It's too awkward."
──────────────────────────────────────────────────
Just remember one Meta-Question.
'Is there anything I might have missed?'
That one question draws out the most.
