=== The Story

The night before the demo.

Seunghyun stood alone in front of the whiteboard.

He looked back over the past month. It had started with a single sentence from Minjun — the TeamOrder notification feature. He remembered thinking about WebSocket within fifteen seconds of first hearing it.

What had been built now was three things.

`FCM` app push and acknowledgement. Night shift urgent `SMS` channel. Notification channel interface.

That was everything.

Compared to everything that had come to mind at the beginning, it was far less. No real-time dashboard. No notification history page. No `Kafka`. The `Kafka` that Jiyeon had wanted to try was nowhere to be found.

_Is this right?_

Seunghyun began writing on the whiteboard.

----
What we understood from Minjun's words at the start:
  "Team members miss tasks and the line stops"
  "Team lead assigns tasks then has to separately message on Slack"
  "Field usage — tablet/phone. Night shift exists"

What we built:
  FCM + acknowledgement → Eliminate "separate messages," reduce "misses"
  SMS fallback → Cover night shift urgent cases
  Channel abstraction → Minimize changes when adding channels later

What we did NOT build and why:
  Notification history → Team lead wanted "did they see it now," not a past log
  Real-time dashboard → Outside demo scope, low risk
  Kafka → Excessive infrastructure for a 15-person team, zero risk now
  Auto reports → Not in requirements. Not a Function in FAC
----

After writing, Seunghyun stared at it for a long time.

_I can see why each of the things we didn't build was okay not to build._

Not building notification history was not laziness. It was because — from the Context-Free Questions in Session 3, what the team lead wanted was "did the team member see it" — current receipt status, not a real-time history of the past.

Not having Kafka was not a technology choice. It was because — the risk check in Session 7 had confirmed there was no risk for a 15-person team right now.

Not having auto reports was because — when FAC was filled in during Session 2, it was never in the Functions list.

_Because we understood the problem, we could see what didn't need to be built._

And because there was so much that didn't need to be built — we could focus on the three things that genuinely mattered. They could be completed within a month.

Seunghyun put down the marker.

Tomorrow at the demo, he would show the team lead these three things. Only three. But Seunghyun knew those three things mapped precisely to what the team lead had said — "I want to stop the line stoppages."

=== Concept Notes

==== When You Understand the Problem, the Solution Becomes Simpler

This is the single proposition that runs through the entire workshop.

[NOTE]
====
When you fully explore the Problem Space, the Solution Space does not become **wider — it becomes narrower**. You don't see more things to build. You see the things you don't need to build.
====

At first, this may seem counterintuitive. Deeply understanding a problem takes more time, and it seems like it would delay entry into the Solution Space.

But in practice, the opposite is true.

When you fully explore the Problem Space, three things become clear.

_First, what must essentially be achieved becomes visible._ What the team lead wanted was "confirmation that team members received their tasks." When that is clear — everything that is not that becomes non-essential.

_Second, what can wait becomes visible._ Things with low risk, things users don't need right this moment. Not building these things now is not laziness — it is judgment.

_Third, what does not need to be built at all becomes identifiable._ Things that were never in FAC's Functions list. Concepts that don't appear in the Domain Model. Things that weren't failure causes in the Pre-mortem. When these become clear, scope naturally contracts.

When these three things are visible — what needs to be built becomes simple. And simple things can be built and delivered to users first.

The reason TeamOrder's three features could reach a demo in one month was not that the rest was lazily skipped. It was that understanding the problem deeply revealed that the rest didn't need to be built.

==== Problem-Solution Fit

While building a solution — or after — there is something that must be confirmed.

Are the problem I defined and the solution I built actually connected?

This is Problem-Solution Fit.

The way to check is simple. What would the user have done without this solution? If the workaround is still there — you missed the core. If the problem can still occur even with this solution — something essential is missing.

What Seunghyun did when he wrote "what we built" and "what we understood from the original problem" side by side and compared them — that was exactly this check.

==== Pre-mortem — Finding Weaknesses Without Optimism Bias

Pre-mortem is a technique proposed by Gary Klein.footnote:[Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," _Harvard Business Review_, 2007. Klein is a decision psychologist who studied how experts form intuitive judgments. Pre-mortem is a technique for removing success bias by imagining future failure before a plan is complete.] It asks: "Assume we failed six months from now — what would the reason be?"

Where an ordinary review looks for "why this will succeed," a Pre-mortem looks for "why this will fail." Without the optimism bias, weaknesses surface more naturally.

The failure reasons that come out of a Pre-mortem are — mostly things that should have been discovered in the Problem Space. A Constraint that wasn't known (Session 2). An Essential Ambiguity that was never Spiked (Session 5). A risk that was underestimated (Session 7). So a Pre-mortem serves as a final check — and as a way to confirm whether the tools from this workshop actually worked.

=== What to Take from This Session

The workshop is ending.

You take the tools with you. FAC, Context-Free Questions, Black Box, layer analysis, Domain Model, Resolvable vs Essential, Spike, analogical reasoning, Risk-Driven design.

But you don't need to pull out all of these tools every time.

There is one habit that all these tools point toward.

When you receive a requirement — before crossing into the Solution Space, pause just once and ask:

_"What do I know right now? And what do I still not know?"_

That question opens the Problem Space. That exploration simplifies the solution. That simplicity enables faster delivery to users.
